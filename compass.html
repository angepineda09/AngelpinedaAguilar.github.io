Project overview

Personal finance tools are often reactive: tracking where money went rather than guiding where it should go. Compass explores how an integrated mobile workflow and an intelligent conversational co-pilot could transform complex banking data into actionable insights and identify a clear next step.

Compass / Product overview01 / Context & Problem

The context

Retail banking applications are built for transaction management, not behavioral guidance. For young users (ages 18–24), digital banking interfaces have abstracted money into numbers on a screen, leading to financial detachment, avoidance, and anxiety.

The Data Behind the Problem

  • Digital Detachment: Over 90% of Gen Z relies entirely on mobile banking and tap-to-pay, causing money to feel abstract and leading to unmanaged cash flow.
  • Impulse Spending ("Vibes"): 57% of young adults identify impulse buying as their primary budget-breaker—making them twice as likely to spend based on emotional impulses rather than plan limits.
  • The Avoidance Loop: 46% of Gen Z adults actively avoid checking their account balances due to financial stress.
  • Lacking Safety Nets: Only 45% of young adults (18–29) can cover a $400 emergency expense without borrowing or taking on debt.
  • Credit Misunderstandings: While most college undergraduates hold credit cards, few understand compound interest or penalty structures, often treating credit as extended cash flow.

Problem Statement

How might we redesign mobile banking for 18–24-year-olds to bridge the gap between digital detachment and long-term financial stability while using conversational AI to intercept impulse spending, demystify credit, and replace financial anxiety with proactive guidance?

Product Opportunity

To solve these specific friction points, Compass was designed with four core capabilities:

  • Behavioral Interception: Proactive, non-judgmental prompts that help users pause before impulse purchases.
  • Contextual Clarity: Translating abstract terms (compound interest, penalty APRs, credit utilization) into simple, real-time feedback.
  • Low-Friction Visibility: A conversational interface that removes the fear of opening the app, letting users ask quick questions without digging through dense account tabs.
  • Adaptive Cash Flow Management: Guidance designed around variable income sources (gig work, campus jobs, parental support).
Compass / Product overview02 / Research & Insights

User Survey

To test assumptions around financial habits and AI sentiment, I conducted a targeted survey (Financial Behavior & AI Agent Survey) with a sample of 10 young adults.

Survey findings

Account Usage & Tracking Behavior

  • 100% of participants actively use checking accounts, and 90% use credit cards.
  • 60% track their spending simply by checking their banking app balance periodically, while 20% don't actively track at all.
  • Takeaway: Most respondents rely on periodic balance checks, suggesting an opportunity for low-friction spending visibility inside their primary banking app.

Saving & Financial Comfort

  • 50% cited that their biggest saving obstacle is that it's "hard to see how small daily spending affects long-term goals".
  • 60% feel moderately comfortable evaluating financial products: they know the basic terms, but comparisons take too much effort. The remaining 40% feel very comfortable; 0% selected uncomfortable or overwhelmed.
  • Takeaway: Compass must bridge the disconnect between daily micro-purchases and macro goals while translating complex rate comparisons into plain language.

AI Trust, Autonomy, & UI Preference

  • 60% preferred a Suggest & Confirm (Human-in-the-Loop) model where the AI presents the plan but requires explicit approval before moving any money. 0% wanted full automation.
  • 80% stated that seeing step-by-step math explanations ("Chain of Thought") would make them trust an AI financial agent.
  • 70% preferred viewing complex financial plans generated as interactive dashboard artifacts/cards rather than scrolling chat threads.
  • Takeaway: These responses suggest prioritizing clear math explanations, explicit user approval, and financial plans presented as pinned dashboard artifacts.

Empathy Mapping

To ground Compass in real user needs, research was synthesized into a comprehensive Empathy Map representing young banking users (ages 18–24), and Santiago, our persona.

What They Hear & See

  • The Noise: Friends practicing "loud budgeting," social media "flex culture" driving money dysmorphia, and constant news on inflation.
  • The Signal: Promotional bank ads that trigger corporate skepticism, counterbalanced by creators advising against Buy Now, Pay Later (BNPL) debt traps.
  • The Reality: Fluctuating account balances, rising prices at checkout, and friends opting for low-cost social alternatives.

What They Say & Do

  • Behavior: Checks balances periodically rather than maintaining complex spreadsheets; relies on "little treats" (e.g., daily coffee) to cope with stress, followed by weekly guilt.
  • Workarounds: Limits social outings ($0 date nights, hosting at home) while managing cash flow across checking accounts, credit cards, and external HYSAs.
  • Stated Intent: "I want to save $150 for Mom's birthday without risking my bill money."

What They Think & Feel

  • Internal Dialogue: "Is this bank card recommendation actually good for my financial health, or is it just a corporate sales pitch?"
  • Core Friction: Financial anxiety when opening banking apps, paired with fear/distrust of unmonitored AI making unauthorized money moves.
  • AI Expectations: Needs to see the exact reasoning (Chain of Thought) and give explicit approval before any transaction occurs.

HMW Challenges

To bridge the gap between the survey findings and the Compass interface, each primary insight was synthesized into a focused design challenge (How Might We) centered around our core user persona, Santiago:

Micro-Spending & Daily Impact

  • Survey Insight: 50% of surveyed users report their biggest savings obstacle is seeing how small daily spending impacts long-term targets, while 41% experience weekly post-purchase guilt.
  • How Might We: How might we help Santiago visualize how small daily purchases affect his short-term savings milestones (e.g., saving $150 for Mom's birthday) without inducing post-purchase guilt or financial anxiety?

Neutrality & Institutional Skepticism

  • Survey Insight: Young adults are deeply skeptical of bank cross-selling and promotion prompts, assuming institutional recommendations serve corporate profits rather than user health.
  • How Might We: How might we position Compass as an objective, unbiased financial co-pilot within the Wells Fargo app that fairly recommends external High-Yield Savings Accounts (HYSAs) or competitor credit cards?

Financial Avoidance & Entry Barriers

  • Survey Insight: 46% of Gen Z adults admit to avoiding checking their bank account balances due to stress and uncertainty, despite 81% stating it is important to be perceived as financially responsible.
  • How Might We: How might we design a calm, non-intrusive entry point that lowers the barrier to checking balances and transforms financial anxiety into proactive financial control?

AI Governance & Safety Guardrails

  • Survey Insight: 0% of surveyed users want fully autonomous AI moving money on its own, 60% demand a "Suggest & Confirm" model, and 80% require step-by-step "Chain of Thought" math explanations before trusting a decision.
  • How Might We: How might we structure a transparent "Suggest / Confirm / Execute" interaction policy with explicit safety guardrails (e.g., auto-pausing transfers if checking drops below $100) so Santiago feels in complete control?

UI Persistence: Artifact Cards vs. Chat Logs

  • Survey Insight: 70% of surveyed target users prefer complex savings roadmaps and budget plans rendered as Interactive Artifact Cards pinned to their dashboard rather than buried in a scrolling chat log.
  • How Might We: How might we design interactive financial Artifact Cards that graduate out of the chat bubble so Santiago can view, edit, and track his 4-week savings goals directly from his main dashboard?
Compass / Product overview03 / Design System

User Task Flow

To establish clear application hierarchy and account for user onboarding edge cases, I mapped the complete decision logic and navigation path for accessing the Compass Agent within the banking app:

Key Architectural Decisions

Low-Friction Onboarding ("First Time User?" Node):

  • If a user is returning, they bypass onboarding and go straight to the Compass Dashboard.
  • If first-time, they pass through a brief User Survey and explicit Permissions Prompt—aligning with our research requirement for transparent user control.

Graceful Permission Handling ("Denied?" Branch):

  • If the user denies AI permissions, the flow loops safely back without locking them out of standard banking tools.
  • When approved, it unlocks full Compass Dashboard capabilities.

Contextual Entry Point ("Need Help" / Dashboard):

  • Accessing Compass is embedded smoothly into daily banking habits from the main home screen rather than hidden deep in setting sub-menus.

Wireframe Sketches

Before jumping into high-fidelity Figma components, I sketched rapid layout concepts to test how the Compass Co-Pilot could be seamlessly integrated across both the Wells Fargo desktop web portal and mobile banking app.

Exploration Focuses

Desktop Website Split-Screen Architecture

  • Seamless Web Integration: Accessible directly via top navigation links ("Help") that transition the user into the dedicated agent dashboard.
  • Cognitive Load Reduction: The web dashboard features quick-action suggestion chips above the text box (e.g., Credit, Budget, Save) to minimize typing friction.
  • The Rightmost Visualization Pane: Unlike the mobile layout, the desktop version leverages a dedicated right-hand column to render live financial visualizations, budget breakdowns, and artifact cards side-by-side with the chat stream.
  • Real-Time Guardrails: The agent explicitly logs its background steps (“Looking at Account 1 from Sept 1st to 30th”) so the user always knows what data is being queried.

Mobile Application Layout & State Management

  • Entry & Navigation: Mobile utilizes a bottom fixed menu for quick help triggers, with a hidden sidebar drawer that expands only when needed.
  • Onboarding & Surveys: First-time users are cleanly routed through a quick goals survey before unlocking the Compass dashboard.
  • Agent Transparency: Even on mobile, the UI surfaces step-by-step agent actions and account analysis breakdowns (e.g., “Analyzing spending from Sep 1st to Sep 30”) to maintain trust.

Low Fidelity

Before applying color or branding, I prototyped the core layout in grayscale to validate the information hierarchy, onboarding friction points, and split-screen desktop behavior.

Interface Architecture

Core Dashboard & Agent Discovery

  • Wells Fargo Integration: The base interface maintains familiar Wells Fargo dashboard styling (Checking, Savings, Visa accounts, and FICO score tracking).
  • Dual Agent Entry: Clear separation between Fargo (reactive chat for quick balance checks) and Compass (proactive co-pilot for budgeting, savings, and credit optimization).

Onboarding Questionnaire & Permission Control

  • Contextual Setup: A 3–5 minute onboarding questionnaire tailored to age group and financial focus.
  • Granular Privacy & Governance: Explicitly asks users which accounts Compass can view and how it handles transfers—reinforcing the research-backed "Suggest & Confirm" model over full automation.

Split-Screen Co-Pilot & "Chain of Thought" Visualization

  • The Desktop Split-Screen: The interface utilizes a left-hand chat stream and a dedicated right-hand visualization pane.
  • Transparent Math ("Thoughts" Drawer): When prompted (e.g., "How much can I put aside for a $500 car repair fund?"), Compass explicitly breaks down account scans, income averages, bill commitments, and discretionary cash flow floors in a readable math trace.
  • Interactive Artifact Generation: The calculated plan graduates into a structured Savings Artifact Card on the right, featuring explicit safety nets (e.g., Stop transfers if checking drops below $300) and clear action buttons (Approve Plan vs. Edit Plan).

High Fidelity

The final UI brings the core research insights to life within the official Wells Fargo web ecosystem, utilizing a structured split-screen desktop layout that balances conversational prompts with persistent financial artifacts.

User Interface

Onboarding & Account Personalization

  • Contextual Setup: Upon entering Compass, first-time users complete a brief 3–5 minute onboarding questionnaire tailored to student/part-time income fluctuations and financial stress points.
  • Explicit Permissions: Users define account access boundaries (e.g., Everyday Checking ...0927, Savings ...6543, Visa ...4603) and select their preferred governance model (Suggest & Confirm), eliminating corporate skepticism and ensuring total user autonomy.

The Dashboard & Proactive Co-Pilot Entry

  • Dual-Agent Architecture: Users can seamlessly transition between Fargo (a reactive chatbot for quick balance checks and bill pay) and Compass (a proactive financial co-pilot for budgeting, micro-savings, and credit optimization).
  • Zero Cognitive Load: Quick-action chips (Calculate Safe-to-Spend, Create Savings Goal, Upcoming Payments) sit directly above the input box to guide user interactions without cluttering the interface.

"Chain of Thought" Math Transparency & Artifact Generation

  • Transparent Reasoning ("Thoughts" Drawer): When Santiago asks to save for a $500 Car Repair Fund, Compass opens an inspectable math breakdown—analyzing 60-day cash flow, monthly income ($1,850), scheduled bills ($1,220), and a $100 safety floor to calculate a safe $45/week transfer target.
  • Interactive Budget Artifact Cards: Rather than burying the plan in a scrolling chat log, the system renders a persistent Budget Proposal card on the right side of the screen.
  • Human-in-the-Loop Safeguards: The artifact explicitly outlines safety nets (e.g., Stop transfers if Everyday Checking drops below $1,120) and requires a manual tap on Approve or Decline before any financial action is executed.
Compass / Product overview04 / Technical Implementation

Compass Technical Implementation

To move beyond static Figma prototypes and evaluate how an AI co-pilot handles real-world constraints, I engineered a functional web prototype powered by the OpenAI API, local Python execution scripts, and synthetic persona data.

Code Generation & Layout Optimization

  • Figma-to-Code Translation: Generated the base HTML/CSS/JS web architecture directly from Figma auto-layout frames using plugin tooling in Codex.
  • Prompt: I am building a responsive website redesign for Wells Fargo. Please use @FIGMA and implement this design.
  • Layout Integrity: Once Codex built the site, I began resolving grid misalignment bugs, repairing broken spacing tokens, and enforcing consistent alignment across the split-screen desktop viewport by communicating with Codex as it adjusted the code.

API Integration & Environment Setup

  • Local Server & Scripting: Once the layout was as desired, I asked Codex to build a local Python agent named Compass to handle state management, user session inputs, and request handling, with the goal of specializing in personal finance.
  • Credential Security: Alongside this, I asked Codex to create a local .env file to store the OpenAI API key used to authenticate the agent's requests.
  • Prompt: Implement an agent in Python called Compass that is an expert financial advisor. The agent belongs in the "Screen - Agent Dashboard Collapsed" frame and should function similarly to popular LLM dashboards. I will provide you with an OpenAI API key for the agent, so provide me with a .env file where I can paste the API key.
  • Key Placement: Once Codex created the .env file, I opened Phoenix Code to paste the OpenAI API key, enabling the agent's financial-advisor functionality.

AGENT BEHAVIOR

System Prompt Refactoring & Guardrail Engineering

  • Overcoming Passive Advice: On initial testing, the default system instructions produced overly passive, generic responses (e.g., standard boilerplate financial tips).
  • Engineering Directives: I redesigned the system prompt to enforce strict operational behavior: Suggest & Confirm governance, mandatory Chain of Thought math steps in the "Thoughts" drawer, and automatic formatting into Artifact Cards, while keeping the agent within its intended scope.

Prompt Reconstruction

  • Original Prompt: You are Compass, a helpful personal finance education assistant in an independent prototype, not Wells Fargo. Be concise, warm, and practical. You can access accounts, transfer funds, or verify current rates; this includes any credit cards in the account. Never invent balances or transactions. Ask for missing amounts before calculating. Explain assumptions. Do not request credentials, account numbers, or identifying information. Use plain text with short paragraphs and simple bullets. Give educational explanations, not personalized investment, legal, or tax advice. Always ask for approvals for any changes.
  • Reconstructed Prompt: You are Compass, a knowledgeable and approachable financial guidance assistant. You will help with budgeting, savings, debt, credit, and financial literacy. Give clear, practical answers with explicit assumptions and checkable calculations. Ask for missing information instead of inventing income, bills, transactions, rates, or risk tolerance. You are an AI, not a licensed advisor or Wells Fargo employee. Never imply live bank access, executed transfers, scheduled payments, or verified market data.

Grounding Data: Santiago’s 3-Month Synthetic Ledger

  • The Cold Start Problem: The agent initially lacked personal context to make specific calculations.
  • Building the Transaction Dataset: Created a detailed 3-month financial spreadsheet (Jan 2026 – Mar 2026) modeling Santiago’s real-world cash flow across both checking and credit card accounts.
  • Forecast Rules & Context Mapping: Configured logic via Codex so Compass treats live dashboard figures as current balances while referencing historical recurring earnings and spending patterns to build future savings roadmaps.

Agent Rules

To ensure Compass operated within strict governance bounds, I established a dedicated rules configuration file (rules.md) inside Phoenix Code. This file served as the operational system prompt loaded directly into the AI agent, dictating data access, financial logic, and user permissions.

Technical Value of the rules.md File

  • Granular Permission Controls: Enforces strict boundary checks. If a user does not grant access to Visa...4603, the agent completely excludes those records rather than defaulting to zero.
  • Double-Counting Avoidance: Solves a major financial AI bug—explicitly preventing credit card repayments from being counted as both a debt payment AND a purchase expense.
  • Action-Gated Proposal Generation: Ensures the agent answers educational questions without prematurely creating financial proposal cards unless explicitly requested.
  • Zero Unintended Money Transfers: Enforces the "Suggest & Confirm" model—confirmations save proposals visually in the "Actions" pane without triggering real automated bank movements.

User Testing

To validate usability and push the limits of the AI's logic, I conducted two rounds of user testing: a "stress test" with a senior designer and task-based testing with 5 participants across diverse backgrounds.

Round 1: Expert Stress Test (Goal: "Break the Agent")

  • Tester: 1 Senior Product Designer.
  • Scenario: Plan a $750 car repair payoff strategy.
  • Critical Edge-Case Discovery: The participant attempted to evaluate paying off the repair using their credit card. While Compass had read-only access to the Visa account, it was unaware of APR interest rates and credit limit mechanics, rendering its credit payoff advice incomplete.
  • Engineering Fixes Implemented:
    1. Credit Logic Integration: Instructed Codex to inject realistic Wells Fargo APR interest rates and credit limit parameters into the Visa account model.
    2. Strict Permission Enforcement: Hardcoded system logic ensuring Compass only queries account data explicitly opted into during the user's onboarding survey.

Round 2: Multi-Disciplinary Testing (N=5) & Navigation Friction

  • Testers: 5 participants across different academic and professional disciplines.
  • Core Insight: While all 5 participants successfully completed financial tasks once inside Compass, only 1 out of 5 was able to find the agent entry point from the main home screen without guidance.
  • Path of Least Resistance Fix: Re-architected the main Wells Fargo homepage entry point using Codex—adding prominent top-level navigation triggers and contextual banners to create a clear, frictionless path to the Compass dashboard.

Compass / Product overview06 / Reflection

Learnings and Next Steps

Key Learnings

  • A useful feature must also be easy to find: During initial testing, users struggled to discover Compass when it was placed under "Help," as that location made it feel like passive customer support rather than a proactive financial planning tool. Moving Compass into the main navigation and adding an entry card on the Home page resolved this friction, establishing a clearer path of least resistance.
  • Financial guidance requires clear context: Testing revealed that spending totals could be misleading when they included checking transactions but excluded credit card purchases. Compass was refined to strictly use accounts selected by the user while distinguishing debit spending from credit spending—reinforcing the importance of explicit data grounding and transparent source information.
  • Recommendations and actions need distinct stages: Users needed a clear transition from discussing a goal to reviewing a proposal. By separating these stages—having Compass explain its recommendation in chat, letting the user request a formal plan, and rendering the proposal in the Actions panel for explicit approval or decline—the interaction model became structured and trustworthy.

Reflection

  • Designing Compass meant designing behavior, not just screens: The interface needed to account for unanticipated user paths, such as choosing to finance a repair with a credit card instead of saving cash upfront. Those edge cases pushed the project beyond standard happy-path screen designs into defining system behavior, handling missing data, and managing risk.
  • A focused prototype prioritized the core experience: While simulated bank transfers were initially explored, they added scope complexity without improving the core validation goals. Narrowing the functional scope to reviewing, understanding, and approving future financial proposals kept the prototype focused on the most critical user-agent interaction loops.
  • Validating future refinements: The next evaluation phase would focus on testing whether unassisted users can consistently discover Compass, verify which accounts inform its answers, and distinguish approving a proposal from executing a live money transfer. Additionally, future tests would measure whether expandable mathematical explanations help users critically assess recommendations rather than blindly accepting them.
