Helping young adults turn everyday money questions into a plan they can understand.

Project overview

An unexpected car repair can turn a simple money question into a stressful decision: use savings, cut spending, or put it on a credit card?

I designed Compass to explore how a conversational assistant could help young adults work through those choices inside a familiar banking experience. Users can ask a question, inspect the calculations behind a suggested plan, and decide whether to approve the proposal.

Independent student concept exploring a Wells Fargo integration. The working prototype uses synthetic account data; approvals save proposals without moving money.

Compass / Product overview01 / Context & Problem

The context

A bank balance tells you what is available now. It doesn’t necessarily tell you what you can set aside after bills, or whether using a credit card would leave you worse off.

I focused on young adults navigating those decisions as they begin managing their own income and expenses. The opportunity was to make the next step easier to understand without taking control away from them.

What the Research Suggested

Checking a balance can be stressful: U.S. Bank and Morning Consult reported that 46% of Gen Z adults said they avoid checking their bank account balances. Source: U.S. Bank ↗

A small emergency can put pressure on a budget: In the Federal Reserve’s 2025 survey, 45% of adults ages 18–29 said they would cover a $400 emergency expense completely using cash or its equivalent. Source: Federal Reserve ↗

These external studies provided context for the project. They are separate from my own 10-person survey, discussed in Research & Insights.

The Design Question

How might we help young adults understand what they can afford, compare their options, and choose a next step while staying in control of their money?

Product Opportunity

I explored a conversational co-pilot within a familiar banking experience that could help users:

  • Ask a money question in everyday language.
  • Review calculations grounded in their selected accounts.
  • Turn a recommendation into a proposal they can inspect and approve.

The prototype tests that interaction using synthetic financial data; it does not execute bank transfers.

Compass / Product overview02 / Research & Insights

User Survey

Before deciding what Compass should do, I shared a survey through my Instagram story and received 10 responses about banking habits, savings challenges, and preferences for AI assistance.

Participants came from my personal network. I used these responses to guide early design decisions rather than represent all young adults.

Survey findings

Checking a balance was the main way respondents tracked spending

  • Finding: Six of 10 checked their banking balance periodically, while two did not actively track spending. All used checking accounts, and nine also used credit cards.
  • Design response: Make relevant account information easy to access without asking users to maintain another budgeting system.

The connection between everyday spending and savings goals was unclear

  • Finding: Five of 10 identified difficulty seeing how small purchases affect longer-term goals as their biggest saving obstacle. Six understood basic financial-product terms but felt comparisons took too much effort.
  • Design response: Connect suggested savings amounts to a concrete goal and explain the calculations in plain language.

Respondents wanted help without giving up control

  • Finding: Six preferred reviewing and confirming an AI-generated plan, while four wanted advice only. None chose full automation. Eight selected calculation breakdowns as something that would increase their trust, and seven preferred plans displayed as persistent dashboard cards.
  • Design response: Let users inspect a recommendation, decide whether to create a proposal, and review it outside the chat before approving.

PERSONA

Santiago Ramirez

Fictional scenario persona informed by survey patterns. Personal details were created to give the prototype a consistent testing context.

  • Age: 22
  • Occupation: College senior and part-time worker
  • Location: Austin, TX
  • Banking setup: Checking account, credit card, and an external high-yield savings account
  • Current habit: Checks balances periodically rather than maintaining a detailed budget

His Situation

Santiago is balancing college, part-time work, and everyday expenses. He checks his balance to understand where he stands, but needs more context before deciding how much he can set aside.

After hitting a post while parallel parking, he faces an unexpected car repair. He needs to compare saving toward the cost with using his credit card, while keeping enough money available for bills.

His Goal

Work out what he can comfortably put toward the repair and understand the tradeoffs before committing to a plan.

What He Needs

To make that decision with confidence, Santiago needs to understand the recommendation and stay in control of what happens next:

  • A recommendation grounded in the accounts he chooses to share.
  • Clear calculations and assumptions he can inspect.
  • Control over whether to approve a proposed plan.

Empathy Mapping

I used the survey patterns to explore Santiago’s situation and identify questions to carry into the prototype.

His thoughts and circumstances are scenario assumptions, not direct participant quotes.

  • What he does: Checks balances periodically and manages spending across more than one account.
  • What he needs to understand: How a repair plan affects bills, available money, and future commitments.
  • What he needs to control: Which accounts inform the answer and whether to approve a proposal.

User Pain Points

The survey suggested three needs to address through Santiago’s repair scenario:

  • Connecting everyday spending to a savings goal.
  • Understanding financial comparisons without excessive effort.
  • Reviewing a recommendation without handing over control.

Design Challenges

I translated those needs into three questions to guide the interface and prototype:

  1. How might we help Santiago understand what he can put toward a repair while keeping money available for bills?
  2. How might we make a recommendation’s calculations, assumptions, and account sources easy to inspect?
  3. How might we help him review and approve a plan without confusing approval with a money transfer?
Compass / Product overview03 / Design Development

User Task Flow

Before designing individual screens, I mapped how someone would reach Compass, what first-time setup would involve, and what should happen if they declined account access.

Key Flow Decisions

  • Returning users: Keep repeat visits short by taking them directly to the dashboard.
  • First-time users: Introduce the setup questions and account permissions before using personal financial context.
  • Declined access: Provide a route back to standard banking rather than trapping users in onboarding.

My initial design placed Compass under Help. Later usability testing challenged that decision: participants struggled to find a planning tool in a location they associated with customer support.

Wireframe Sketches

I started with sketches to explore how Compass could fit into the Wells Fargo website and mobile app. The main question was how to keep a conversation, account context, and a proposed plan accessible without crowding the screen.

Exploration Focuses

Desktop: keeping the conversation and plan together

  • Room to compare: I explored placing the conversation beside a separate panel for financial summaries and proposals. This would let Santiago review a plan without searching through earlier messages.
  • A starting point: Suggested prompts such as Credit, Budget, and Save gave users examples of what they could ask.
  • Visibility during a request: I sketched how Compass could show which account and time period it was reviewing, so users could follow the request.

Mobile: prioritizing one task at a time

  • More space for the conversation: I explored hiding secondary navigation in a drawer, keeping the main screen focused on the current task.
  • Access to supporting information: Account details and financial summaries needed to remain reachable without competing with the conversation.
  • A shared interaction model: Both layouts kept the same intended sequence: ask a question, understand the recommendation, and review a proposal before approving it.

These sketches captured early layout ideas. The grayscale prototype was the next step in exploring how they would work on screen.

Low Fidelity

I translated the sketches into grayscale screens to work through the journey from opening Compass to reviewing a financial proposal. Leaving out color and branding helped me focus on what information belonged together and what users needed at each step.

Interface Architecture

Giving Compass a place within everyday banking

  • A familiar starting point: I kept the account overview as the entry to the experience, then explored how users would discover Compass alongside Fargo.
  • Different purposes: Fargo handled quick banking questions, while Compass focused on working through a financial goal. The early design introduced both tools before users entered the conversation.

Establishing context before offering guidance

  • First-time setup: The onboarding screens introduced questions about the user’s financial situation and goals.
  • Account selection: Users could choose which accounts Compass could reference. This made the intended data boundaries part of the experience rather than an invisible assumption.

Separating a conversation from a proposed plan

  • Ask and clarify: The conversation gave Santiago space to describe his repair goal and provide the context needed for a recommendation.
  • Inspect the calculations: An expandable breakdown offered a place to review the amounts and assumptions behind the suggested plan.
  • Review a proposal: A separate panel kept the plan visible beside the conversation, with controls to approve or revise it.

This stage established the screen structure and intended interaction sequence. The later working prototype let me investigate how those interactions behaved with generated responses and synthetic account data.

High Fidelity

With the screen structure in place, I brought the interface into the visual language of Wells Fargo’s website. I used familiar account cards, navigation, and colors to make Compass feel connected to the banking experience, while giving the conversation and proposed plan distinct spaces.

User Interface

Helping users start a conversation

  • A familiar entry: Users begin with their account overview and can access Compass for help working through a financial goal.
  • Suggested questions: Prompts such as Calculate Safe-to-Spend, Create Savings Goal, and Upcoming Payments offer starting points for users who may not know what to ask.
  • Relevant account context: Account selection makes it visible which information Compass can use when responding.

Making a recommendation easier to inspect

  • Conversation first: Santiago can describe his car repair goal and ask follow-up questions before requesting a proposal.
  • Calculations on demand: The expandable “Thoughts” drawer provides a place to inspect the amounts and assumptions behind the recommendation without filling the main conversation with every detail.
  • A plan that stays visible: The proposal appears in a separate panel beside the chat, so users can review it without searching through earlier messages.

Making the approval step explicit

  • A distinct decision point: Approve and Decline controls separate reviewing a proposal from agreeing to it.
  • Visible conditions: The proposal includes the suggested savings amount and balance conditions, giving users specific details to examine before deciding.
Compass / Product overview04 / Technical Implementation

Building a Working Prototype

Static screens showed how Compass should look, but they couldn’t show how it would respond when someone asked an unexpected question. I used Codex to help build a working web prototype, then explored its responses using Santiago’s synthetic financial data.

My role was to define the interaction, review the generated implementation, and refine the experience when the agent’s answers or the interface fell short.

Translating the design into an interactive experience

  • Starting from Figma: I used Codex and Figma tooling to generate the initial web interface from my designs.
  • Reviewing the result: I checked the generated screens against the intended layout and directed adjustments to spacing, alignment, and the split-screen arrangement.
  • Connecting the conversation: With Codex’s assistance, I connected the interface to a local Python agent using the OpenAI API. This let me explore questions and follow-up responses beyond a fixed prototype path.

AGENT BEHAVIOR

The early instructions combined my input with Codex-generated wording. During testing, I noticed that Compass responded as though it could act on real accounts. Its instructions described account access and transfers without clearly defining the limits of the prototype.

I revised the instructions to distinguish the synthetic account information Compass could reference from actions it could not perform.

  • Ground answers in available information: Ask for missing details rather than inventing balances, transactions, or financial terms.
  • Make recommendations inspectable: Explain the calculations and assumptions behind a suggested plan.
  • State the limits: Do not imply live bank access, completed transfers, scheduled payments, or verified market rates.

The intended interaction remained useful without pretending to execute banking actions: Compass could discuss a goal and help prepare a proposal for the user to review.

Compass needed more than a starting balance to respond to Santiago’s repair question. I created a three-month synthetic ledger to give the prototype a consistent financial scenario to work with.

  • A realistic testing context: The spreadsheet covered January through March 2026, with income, everyday expenses, and transactions across checking and credit card accounts.
  • History and current balances: I used Codex to help configure how Compass referenced transaction history alongside the balances displayed in the prototype. Past earnings and spending provided context for discussing what Santiago might set aside.
  • A consistent basis for iteration: Using the same fictional data let me explore different questions without connecting real bank accounts.

Agent Rules

I collected the agent’s operating instructions in a rules.md file inside Phoenix Code. This gave me one place to refine how Compass should use account information, explain spending, and respond when someone requested a plan.

The instructions addressed four recurring concerns:

  • Respect account selection: Treat an unselected account as unavailable, not as an account with a zero balance. The instructions told Compass not to use or reveal information from accounts the user had not selected.
  • Avoid counting the same spending twice: Distinguish purchases from credit card repayments so moving money to pay a card would not also appear as a new purchase expense.
  • Keep questions separate from proposals: Answer informational questions without creating a plan card automatically. Create a proposal when the user requests or agrees to one.
  • Clarify what approval means: In this prototype, approval saves a proposal in the Actions panel. It does not execute a bank transfer.

These instructions defined the intended behavior. They were not a substitute for application-level permission checks or testing the agent’s responses.

User Testing

I tested the working prototype in two rounds: an exploratory session with a senior product designer, followed by task-based sessions with five participants from different academic and professional backgrounds. I wanted to understand both where the agent’s guidance fell short and whether people could reach and use it.

An unexpected question exposed missing financial context

  • The task: A senior product designer explored how to plan for a $750 car repair and considered using a credit card rather than saving the full amount upfront.
  • What I observed: Compass could reference the synthetic Visa account, but its account model lacked APR and credit-limit details. That left it without important context for comparing a credit-funded option.
  • What I changed: I used Codex to help add example APR and credit-limit parameters to the synthetic account model and refine account-selection handling so the prototype used only the accounts the user had chosen to share.
  • What I learned: Supporting a savings scenario was not enough. The experience also needed to account for alternative choices and make missing information visible rather than treating an incomplete answer as sufficient.

People could use Compass, but struggled to find it

  • The sessions: Five participants attempted financial tasks using the prototype.
  • What I observed: All five completed the financial tasks once inside Compass, but only one found its entry point from the home screen without guidance.
  • What I changed: I moved Compass into the main navigation and added a contextual Home-page banner with an Ask Compass button, creating more direct entry points than the original Help route.
  • What remains to test: Whether new participants can discover Compass without assistance and understand that approving a proposal does not move money.

These sessions identified issues to address in the prototype. A follow-up evaluation is still needed to assess the revised entry points.

Compass / Product overview06 / Reflection

Reflection and Next Steps

Key Learnings

  • A useful feature must also be easy to find: Only one of five participants found Compass from the home screen without guidance. This showed me that the entry point was part of the experience, not a detail to resolve after designing the agent. I added direct navigation and a Home-page banner, but those changes still need a follow-up test.
  • Financial guidance depends on its context: The credit-card repair scenario exposed missing APR and credit-limit information. I learned to question what data supported an answer and to make account sources, assumptions, and missing information visible.
  • Designing an AI experience means designing behavior: Screens alone could not account for unexpected questions or an agent overstating its capabilities. Building the prototype helped me see why a conversation, a recommendation, and approval of a proposal needed distinct stages.
  • A focused scope made the project more useful to evaluate: I narrowed the prototype to discussing goals, inspecting calculations, and reviewing proposals rather than simulating money transfers. That kept the work centered on the interaction I wanted to explore.

Next Steps

  • Retest discovery: Ask new participants to find Compass from the home screen without guidance and observe whether the revised navigation and banner help them reach it.
  • Check understanding of permissions and approval: Ask participants to identify which accounts inform a response and explain what approving a proposal does. Use any confusion to refine labels and feedback.
  • Evaluate the calculation breakdowns: Test whether participants can identify the assumptions behind a recommendation, notice missing information, and question an unsuitable plan rather than simply accepting the answer.
